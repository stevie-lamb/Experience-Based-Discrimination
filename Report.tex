\begin{abstract}
	This paper aims to construct an Agent Based Model of the labour market via a contextual bandit problem. The paper seeks to uncover mechanisms by which discrimination in the labour market occurs, and subsequently, policy that can remedy said mechanisms. While the model does not fully explain discrimination in the labour market, reflections upon it's results provide useful insight.
	
	The contribution of this paper is to show that by not creating a model of equilibrium, wage distributions can be generated the more realistically replicate empirical reality, and thus provide further insight on the consequences of Experience Based Discrimination
\end{abstract} %%%%%%%%%

\section*{Literature Review}

\subsection*{Discrimination}
\subsubsection*{Theory}

\citet{Lepage2024} develops a model of Experience Based Discrimination. The paper, which largely motivates the development of the model presented in this paper, posits that firms are not only uncertain over individual productivities, but also the productivity of the group. Typically economic literature has compartmentalised discrimination into two main categories, \textit{taste-based}, and \textit{statistical} discrimination. The former, associated with Becker (1957), corresponds to discrimination purely because the hiring manager prefers (or dislikes) some group. The latter is predicated on the assumption that firms are not inherently discriminatory, but are discriminatory in practice due to failures in ability to read signals. The Statistical Discrimination literature is associated with the work of \citet{Arrow1973} and \citet{phelpsstat}. Here, discrimination arrives because of \textit{"scarcity of information about the existence and characteristics of workers and jobs"}. Thus, the 'Experience Based Discrimination' is closely related to the statistical discrimination literature. However, there is a large difference, as noted by \citet{Lepage2024}, Statistical Discrimination relies on some inherent difference in the characteristics of groups, while Experience Based Discrimination does not.

Other models such as that of \citet{LunderbergStartz1983} utilise endogenous human capital decisions to engender discrimination; if workers from minority groups expect to receive a lower wage for their efforts, then they will underinvest in human capital, creating a divergence in productivity between groups. The model, however, relies on innate differences on reliability of observable test scores upon which wages are based.
and \citet{CoatesLoury1993}, show that perverse employer beliefs can cause minority groups  to underinvest in human capital, which creates divergence between group productivities.


\subsubsection*{Empirics}	

\citet{LiRaymondBergman2020} use firm-level recruitment data to test ML algorithms in hiring policy. Due to a lack of data on performance, the data objective function is to maximise hiring potential conditional on being interviewed; they find that algorithms that algorithms that understand the trade-off between exploration and exploitation perform much better on metrics of fairness and efficiency of hiring policy than 'supervised' ML algorithms focus on exploitation.
\citet{Lepage2024} uses an expiremental labour market design to observe experience based discrimination; they too find evidence that endogenous learning can create discrimination.
Both of these papers set up their empirics as a 'Multi-Armed Bandit' problem.

\subsection*{Multi Armed Bandit Problems}

Multi Armed Bandit (MAB) problems have a strong history in economics; MABs are usually applied to problems characterised by a trade-off between exploration and exploitation \citet{BergemannValimaki2006}, that is, executing an action we currently believe to be optimal, versus exploring the action space to understand whether some other action is optimal.
The Contextual bandit problem is similar, but the agent observes a an N-dimensional \textit{feature} vector, providing context over each arm.

\section*{Model}

\subsubsection*{Workers}

There are $N_w$ workers in the set of workers $W$, each belonging to a group $G \in g = 1, 2, ..., G$, such that $\bigcup_{g=1}^{G} G_g = W$, and $G_i \cap G_j = \emptyset \quad \forall i, j \in \{1, 2, \dots, G\}$ where $ i \neq j$. 

\vspace{0.1cm}

Workers have an innate ability, which constitutes their marginal productivity, but is unobservable to firms, and a purely stochastic signal that is observable to the firms. Although workers in the model are heterogeneous, the underlying distributions from which their productivities and signals are drawn are homogenous.

\begin{center}
$Productivity \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_g, \sigma_{pg}^2)$
	
	\vspace*{0.2cm}
	
	$Signal \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{sg}^2)$
\end{center}
With a mean of 0, the signal on the workers' productivity is unbiased.

Then:
\begin{center}
	$Productivity = ability + \epsilon_{pg}$
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	
	$Signal = Productivity + \epsilon_{sg} = ability + \epsilon_{pg} + \epsilon_{sg}$
	
\end{center}

Unlike other models of statistical discrimination, the nature of endogenous learning within the model does not necessitate the groups of workers to be inherently different.

Then:
\begin{center}
	$\mu_g = \mu$, \hspace*{0.2cm}
	$ \sigma_{pg}^2 = \sigma_p$,\hspace*{0.2cm}
	$ \sigma_{sg}^2 = \sigma_s$ \hspace*{0.2cm} $ \forall g$
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{Firms}

Each firm faces a 'Multi Armed Bandit problem; at each time period $t$, the firm matches with a number of prospective employees from the worker group, and they must decide which 'arm' (worker) to employ for the coming period. The firm must pull the arm (employ the worker) that they believe will provide them with the greatest profit. 
The problem becomes a 'Contextual Bandit' problem because the firm observes a \textit{feature vector} describing the nature of the prospective workers: they observe both the group they belong to, and the signal on their productivity.

The firm selects worker $i$ to maximise expected profit $\pi$:

\begin{center}
	$\operatorname*{argmax}_i \pi = \mathbb{E}(\mu_i) - w_i$
\end{center}

The firms are modelled as \textit{Bayesian Updaters}: they have a prior distribution over both the distribution of productivities, and of signals for each group. If firms were \textit{frequentist}, their expectations would be biased heavily towards the few observations they have. Modelling firms as Bayesian makes sense as they already have a sensible estimate of the distribution of the group's productivity distribution from the outset.

The firms correctly model productivity and signals as being normally distributed. For productivity, the firms have uncertainty over both the mean and the variance of the true distribution. Then the conjugate prior for this distribution is $NormalInverseGamma$.

\begin{center}
	$P_g | \mu_g, \sigma_{pg}^2\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_{pg})$
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	$\mu | \sigma_{pg}^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \frac{\sigma_{pg}^2}{\eta_0} )$
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	$\sigma_{pg}^2 \sim IG(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$
	
\end{center}

\begin{center}
	$\pi(P_{new}|P, \mu_i, \sigma_{pg}, \alpha_0, \beta_0) = p(P)$

\end{center}

Although the groups have identical parameters, firms have a set of prior distributions, and thus posterior distributions for each worker group.

In the model firms are assumed to have a strong degree of monopsony power; since matches only last one period, workers cannot reject in order to continue search, as they will remain unemployed for that period if they reject. Then firms determine the wage entirely.
Firms use a weight average of the expected productivity of an individual, and the observable signal. The weight is determined by the variance of each of these, as given by the firms current posterior distribution. Since the firms update their priors over each group, the wage is determined partly by their group membership: if the firm is uncertain about the group's productivity, they will more heavily weight the signal, and vice versa.

\begin{center}
	$w_{ig} = \phi * \mathbb{E}[\mu_g] + (1 - \phi) * signal_i$
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	$\phi = \dfrac{\sigma_{sg}}{\sigma_{sg}  + \sigma_{pg}}$
\end{center}
Then as $\sigma_{pg} \to \infty, \phi \to 0$ and we put no weight on the group's productivity distribution. While as $\sigma_{pg} \to 0, \phi \to 1$ and we put full weight on the group's productivity because firm believes the productivity of the group has a mass fully centered on the mean.

\subsection*{Algorithmic Fairness}

A key point that this model provides guidance on is that of Algorithmic Fairness. If employers are to use Machine Learning to decide who to hire in their hiring policy, this could lead to a bias analogous to that presented in this paper; underlying data inherently contains the kernel of human bias, and carries this forward. This highlights the importance of policy intervention, and underpins tha the common problem that Reinforcement Learning seeks to solve: one of \textit{exploration} vs \textit{exploitation}.
